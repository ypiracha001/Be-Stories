A handbag carries things.

A watch tells time.

A hotel provides somewhere to sleep.

A bottle contains fragrance.

Described by function, much of luxury makes very little economic sense.

And yet people routinely pay extraordinary premiums for objects whose practical purpose could be satisfied for a fraction of the price.

The obvious explanation is quality.

It is also incomplete.

Materials matter. Craft matters. Provenance matters.

But somewhere between what an object does and what someone is willing to pay for it, something less tangible is happening.

Desire is creating economic value.

The object is only the beginning

Consider what happens before someone owns something they deeply desire.

They notice it.

Then recognise it.

Perhaps encounter it again.

They imagine themselves with it.

They understand something about the world surrounding it.

Eventually, the object begins to represent more than its function.

A place.

A memory.

A version of oneself.

Belonging.

Taste.

Achievement.

Pleasure.

The physical product has not changed.

Its meaning has.

And markets routinely assign enormous value to that difference.

The distance between utility and value is often filled by meaning.

This is why reducing luxury marketing to product communication misses the more interesting economic function of brand.

The task is not simply to tell people what something is.

It is to create the conditions under which an object becomes wanted.

Desire needs distance

Modern commerce is obsessed with removing friction.

One click.

Same-day delivery.

Infinite inventory.

Constant communication.

Immediate access.

For most businesses, this is progress.

Desire behaves differently.

We tend to want things partly because there is some distance between us and them.

Physical distance.

Financial distance.

Cultural distance.

Temporal distance.

Sometimes simply imaginative distance.

This does not mean making customers’ lives unnecessarily difficult.

It means recognising that anticipation has value.

The restaurant table that requires planning feels different from the one permanently available.

The object revealed slowly can acquire more weight than the object shown from every angle immediately.

The brand that does not explain every part of itself leaves something for the audience to complete.

Luxury therefore contains a strange commercial tension:

make purchasing effortless without making desire effortless.

Recognition compounds

Some of the world’s strongest brands can be recognised from fragments.

A colour.

A silhouette.

A clasp.

A pattern.

A particular photographic temperature.

A few notes of sound.

An unusual amount of empty space.

These codes did not become valuable because somebody put them into a brand guideline.

They became valuable because the brand invested in them repeatedly.

Recognition accumulated.

This is one reason relentless reinvention can be expensive.

Marketing culture prizes novelty. Brands often feel pressure to arrive every season with a completely new visual world.

But memory needs repetition.

The challenge is not to repeat executions.

It is to repeat meaning without becoming repetitive.

That distinction is where brand equity begins to compound.

Consistency preserves recognition. Evolution preserves attention. Great brands manage both.

Price is part of the story

Price is usually discussed as the result of value.

In luxury, it can also participate in creating it.

An expensive object makes a claim.

About materials.

About scarcity.

About craft.

About status.

About who it imagines its customer to be.

That claim then has to be supported by everything surrounding the object.

This is where brands sometimes create a contradiction.

The product says rare.

The communication says everywhere.

The price says considered.

The content says urgent.

The store says timeless.

The social feed says please don’t forget about us.

Each individual decision may be rational.

Together, they can erode the very premium the business is trying to defend.

Attention and desire are not synonyms

A spectacular image can earn attention.

A controversy can earn more.

A trend can produce extraordinary reach.

None guarantees desire.

Attention asks:

Did they look?

Desire asks:

Did looking change the value of the thing in their mind?

Those are different economic questions.

This distinction becomes especially important when marketing systems optimise around what can be measured quickly.

Clicks arrive quickly.

Views arrive quickly.

Conversions can sometimes arrive quickly.

Brand meaning often does not.

It accumulates across encounters until the customer cannot point to the precise moment their preference formed.

They simply know which one they want.

The most expensive signal may be restraint

There is an instinct, particularly when media is expensive, to make every second work harder.

More product.

More copy.

More branding.

More reasons to believe.

More information.

But density does not necessarily create value.

Sometimes confidence is communicated by what a brand appears comfortable leaving out.

An image with room around the object.

A film willing to hold a shot.

Packaging without eleven claims on the front.

A campaign that trusts one thought.

A brand that does not participate in every conversation available to it.

Restraint can signal that attention is expected rather than begged for.

That signal is difficult to quantify.

It is also difficult to fake.

When everything is asking to be noticed, composure becomes conspicuous.

Technology changes supply, not human nature

Artificial intelligence changes the economics of creative production profoundly.

Images that once required elaborate shoots can be explored rapidly.

Worlds can be extended.

Iterations multiplied.

Personalisation becomes more plausible.

Production constraints continue to fall.

This changes supply.

It does not necessarily change desire.

People still notice.

Anticipate.

Compare.

Remember.

Signal.

Imagine.

Want.

The technological opportunity, then, is not merely to flood the market with everything it has become possible to make.

It is to use expanded creative possibility in service of the same old human mysteries.

Why this object?

Why this brand?

Why now?

Why does seeing it make me want to move closer?

Desire is designed

Not controlled.

Not manufactured on command.

Designed.

Through thousands of decisions that alter the context in which something is encountered.

The space around an object.

The pace of a film.

The weight of a word.

The frequency with which a symbol appears.

The decision to reveal.

The decision to withhold.

The consistency of a world.

The moments when that world is allowed to change.

None of these guarantees desire.

Human beings are more complicated than that.

But together they influence perceived meaning.

And perceived meaning influences preference.

Preference influences willingness to pay.
